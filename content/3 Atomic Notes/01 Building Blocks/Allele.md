> [!abstract] In one sentence
> An allele is one version of a gene; different alleles of the same gene differ slightly in DNA sequence and can produce different traits.

> [!example] Explain it like I'm new (the Feynman way)
> Alleles are different versions of the same recipe, like Grandma's chocolate cake vs. a version where one ingredient got misprinted. Same dish, slightly different result.

## Build it from first principles

Slide 3: an allele is 'a form or variety of a gene, result of a mutation'.

- Long ago a [[Mutation|mutation]] changed the DNA of the kernel-color gene in some corn plant. Now two versions exist in corn populations: one that makes purple pigment (**R**) and one that doesn't (**r**).
- Each individual carries **two** alleles for each gene (one from each parent), but a population can contain many alleles of a gene (see [[Multiple Alleles]]).
- The pair of alleles an individual carries is its [[Genotype|genotype]].

Alleles are written as letters: the same letter for the same gene, capital for [[Dominant Allele|dominant]] and lowercase for [[Recessive Allele|recessive]] (see [[Allele Notation]]).

## Where it shows up in Lab 7

R (purple) and r (yellow) are two alleles of the kernel-color gene; H (smooth) and h (wrinkled) are two alleles of the kernel-shape gene; G (green) and g (albino) are alleles of a seedling-color gene.

> [!question]- Test yourself: Can one person carry three alleles of a gene?
> **Answer:** No. Each person has two copies, though the population can have many alleles (e.g. blood type has three).
