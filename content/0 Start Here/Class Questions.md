These are the questions students actually ask in a genetics lab like this one, with answers you can say out loud. Each answer reasons from the basics, the way you'd think it through at the bench, and links to the notes that hold the full idea. Cover the answer, try it yourself, then check.

## Basics: genes, alleles and dominance

**"What's the difference between a gene and an allele?"**
A gene is the recipe slot, like "kernel color". An allele is the version of the recipe in that slot: R (purple) or r (yellow). Everyone has the kernel-color gene; plants differ in which alleles they hold. See [[Gene]], [[Allele]].

**"Why do we have two letters for each trait?"**
Because you have two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent, so two copies of each gene. See [[Why you have two copies of every gene]].

**"If dominant alleles win, why don't they take over the population?"**
Dominant only means one copy shows in a single individual. How common an allele is depends on history and selection. Huntington is dominant and rare. See [[Why dominant does not mean common]].

**"What does dominant actually *do* to the recessive allele?"**
Nothing. Both alleles are working away (or not). The recessive one is usually a broken recipe, and one working copy makes enough product to look normal. See [[Why the working allele is usually dominant]].

**"Is a recessive allele a bad allele?"**
Not necessarily. "Recessive" means only that its effect is hidden by one dominant copy. Blood type O is recessive and perfectly healthy. See [[Recessive Allele]].

**"Can two parents with the dominant trait have a child with the recessive one?"**
Yes, if both are heterozygous. Each passes the hidden recessive allele ¼ of the time together. That's how albino seedlings come from green parents. See [[Why the recessive trait comes back in the F2]].

**"What's the difference between genotype and phenotype?"**
Genotype is the letters (Gg); phenotype is what you see (green). Different genotypes can give the same phenotype (GG and Gg both look green). See [[Genotype]], [[Phenotype]].

**"Why does one gene make a kernel purple? Where's the color coming from?"**
The gene is a recipe for an enzyme; the enzyme makes the pigment anthocyanin. No working enzyme, no pigment, yellow shows. See [[Topic - From DNA to Trait]], [[Anthocyanin]].

## Meiosis and Punnett squares

**"Why does each gamete get only one allele?"**
Meiosis separates the two homologous chromosomes into different gametes, so each gets one of the pair. See [[Why a gamete carries only one allele of each gene]].

**"Why do the eggs go on top and the sperm down the side?"**
It doesn't matter mathematically; it's a convention so everyone reads squares the same way. The TA tip: egg from Mom on top, sperm from Dad down the side. See [[Punnett Square]].

**"Why are all four boxes equally likely?"**
Each parent makes each gamete type 50:50, and which egg meets which sperm is random, like two coin flips. See [[Why every box in a Punnett square is equally likely]].

**"If the square says ¼, will exactly 1 in 4 of my children have it?"**
No. ¼ is the chance for **each** child separately. A family of four could have zero or four affected. See [[Why each birth is an independent event]].

**"How do I write the gametes for RrHh?"**
Each gamete gets one R-gene letter and one H-gene letter. Use FOIL: RH, Rh, rH, rh. See [[FOIL Method]], [[Calc 02 - Writing Gametes]].

**"Why is the dihybrid ratio 9:3:3:1?"**
Each gene alone gives 3:1. The genes sort independently, so multiply: (3+1)(3+1) = 9+3+3+1. See [[Why a dihybrid cross gives 9 3 3 1]].

**"Do I always need a 16-box square?"**
No. Solve each gene separately and multiply with the product rule. ¾ purple × ¾ smooth = 9/16. See [[Product Rule]].

**"Why does a test cross use the recessive parent?"**
aa only gives a, so it hides nothing: every offspring's phenotype shows the mystery parent's allele directly. See [[Why a test cross uses a homozygous recessive partner]].

**"What's the difference between genotypic and phenotypic ratio?"**
Genotypic counts letter combinations (1 GG : 2 Gg : 1 gg); phenotypic counts appearances (3 green : 1 albino). See [[Genotypic Ratio]], [[Phenotypic Ratio]].

## Patterns of inheritance

**"Isn't incomplete dominance just blending?"**
No. Cross two pinks and red and white come back. The alleles never mixed; one copy just makes half the pigment. See [[Why incomplete dominance is not blending]].

**"How do I tell incomplete dominance from codominance?"**
Incomplete: the heterozygote is in between (pink). Codominance: both versions show fully (AB blood, speckled chickens). See [[Why codominance and incomplete dominance are different]].

**"Why are more men color-blind than women?"**
Men have one X; one recessive allele is enough. Women need two. See [[Why X-linked traits show up more in males]].

**"Can a color-blind father have a color-blind son?"**
Not from him: sons get Dad's Y, not his X. A son would be color-blind only if his mother passed an Xᵇ. See [[Color Blindness]].

**"Why is sickle cell called recessive and codominant and incompletely dominant?"**
Because it depends on what level you look at: the person's health (recessive), the proteins made (codominant), the cells at low oxygen (incomplete). See [[Topic - Sickle Cell One Gene Many Patterns]].

**"If sickle cell is harmful, why hasn't evolution removed it?"**
Carriers resist malaria, so where malaria is common they leave more children. See [[Why the sickle cell allele survives]].

## The corn lab

**"Why corn?"**
One ear holds hundreds of offspring of a single controlled cross, with traits visible on the kernels. See [[Corn]].

**"Is each kernel a separate baby?"**
Yes. Each kernel is one fertilization: one pollen grain met one ovule through one silk. See [[Kernel]], [[Corn Ear]].

**"Why are some seedlings white?"**
They're gg: no working chlorophyll gene, so no chlorophyll. They live on stored seed food, then die. See [[Albino Seedling]].

**"How do we know who the parents were if we never saw them?"**
Albinos (gg) mean both parents carry g. Test 3:1 (Gg × Gg) and 1:1 (Gg × gg) with chi-square, and see which fits. See [[Topic - Who Were the Parents]], [[Why an albino seedling means both parents carry g]].

**"Why only five rows? Wouldn't the whole ear be more accurate?"**
Slightly, but five rows (100-250 kernels) is already a solid sample, and it leaves time for the math. See [[Why we count five rows instead of the whole ear]].

**"Why are wrinkled kernels wrinkled?"**
hh can't turn sugar into starch; sugar pulls in water, which dries out and leaves wrinkles. They're the sweet ones. See [[Why wrinkled kernels are sweet]].

**"My ear 2 numbers don't match the table on p. 131."**
The second table labeled "Corn Ear 1" on p. 131 is really ear 2. See [[Worked - Corn Ear 2 Test Cross]].

## Chi-square

**"My counts don't match 3:1. Did we do it wrong?"**
Almost certainly not. Chance makes real counts wobble; the test checks whether the wobble is normal. See [[Why real counts never match the prediction exactly]].

**"Why do we need chi-square? Can't we just look?"**
Eyes see what they hope for. The test is a fair, shared rule for "is this gap chance-sized?". See [[Why we need a statistical test at all]].

**"What is the null hypothesis, in plain words?"**
"There's no real difference between what we counted and what the ratio predicts; any gap is chance." See [[Null Hypothesis]], [[Why the null hypothesis says there is no difference]].

**"How do I get the expected numbers?"**
Expected fraction × total counted. 23 seedlings at 3:1: ¾ × 23 = 17.25 and ¼ × 23 = 5.75. Keep the decimals. See [[Calc 06 - Expected Counts]].

**"Why square the differences?"**
The raw differences always add to zero. Squaring makes them all positive. See [[Why we square the gaps in chi-square]].

**"Why divide by expected?"**
A gap of 5 matters more when you expected 10 than when you expected 1,000. See [[Why we divide by the expected count]].

**"Do I take the square root of the sum?"**
No. The "Sum =" line **is** χ². See [[Chi-Square Statistic]].

**"Where do degrees of freedom come from?"**
Categories minus one: once the total is fixed, the last category is forced. See [[Why degrees of freedom are categories minus one]].

**"Is a bigger χ² better?"**
No. Bigger means a worse fit. See [[Why a big chi-square means a poor fit]].

**"My χ² is 0.43. That's bigger than 0.05, so I reject, right?"**
No. Compare with the critical value (3.841 for df 1), not with 0.05. 0.43 < 3.841, so you fail to reject. See [[Why we compare chi-square with the critical value not with 0.05]].

**"Why can't I say I accept the null hypothesis?"**
A small χ² means the data is consistent with the ratio, not that it's proved. Other ratios might fit too. Say "fail to reject" (or "accept provisionally"). See [[Why we never accept the null hypothesis]].

**"Our tray fits 3:1, but it also fits 2:1. Which is right?"**
With 23 seedlings, chance is wide enough to cover both. Genetics says 3:1 (Gg × Gg); class data narrows it down. See [[Why one table can fit two hypotheses at once]].

**"Can I use percentages instead of counts?"**
No. χ² depends on sample size; percentages hide it. See [[Why chi-square needs counts not percentages]].

**"Why does ear 5 have a different critical value?"**
Four categories → df 3 → bar 7.815. See [[Why ear 5 uses a different critical value]].

**"Why does the class data give a much bigger χ² for 1:1 than our table did?"**
Bigger samples make real differences stand out: 7.348 from 23 seedlings vs 37.029 from 140. See [[Why a bigger sample gives a clearer answer]].

**"One table rejected 3:1 but everyone else didn't. Is their corn different?"**
Probably not. At 0.05, about 1 table in 20 rejects a true hypothesis by chance. Recheck their counts too. See [[Why about 1 in 20 honest tables will still reject]].

## Disorders and mitochondria

**"Two healthy parents have a child with cystic fibrosis. How?"**
Both are carriers (Aa). Each child has a ¼ chance of aa. See [[Autosomal Recessive Disorder]].

**"Why do we get mitochondria only from our mothers?"**
The egg brings all the cytoplasm and its mitochondria; sperm mitochondria are destroyed. See [[Why mitochondria come only from the mother]].

**"Why do mitochondria have their own DNA?"**
They were once free-living bacteria swallowed by an ancient cell. See [[Endosymbiotic Theory]].

**"They've had four girls. Isn't a boy due?"**
No. Each birth is ½. (½)⁵ = 1/32 is the chance of five girls **planned from the start**, not the chance of the fifth. See [[Worked - Study Guide Q9 Five Girls]].

## Tricky questions to be ready for

**"Do the genes for kernel color and shape ever stay together?"**
If two genes sit close on the same chromosome they tend to be inherited together ([[Gene Linkage]]), and the 9:3:3:1 breaks down. Lab 7 assumes they're on different chromosomes. A strongly rejected 9:3:3:1 could be a hint of linkage.

**"Is everything controlled by one gene like this?"**
No. Height, skin color and yield are [[Polygenic Trait|polygenic]] and shaped by environment. Lab 7 chooses single-gene traits because they give clean ratios.

**"Why is the significance level 0.05 and not something else?"**
It's a convention: a balance between false alarms ([[Type I Error]]) and missed differences ([[Type II Error]]).

**"Could a mistake in counting change the conclusion?"**
Yes, especially in small categories (like yellow-wrinkled on ear 5), because dividing by a small E magnifies errors. See [[Assumptions of Chi-Square]].
