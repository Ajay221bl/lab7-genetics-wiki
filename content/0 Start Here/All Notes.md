## Molecular notes · Why Notes (33)

- [[Why X-linked traits show up more in males]]: X-linked recessive traits show up more in males because males have only one X: a single recessive allele there has no second copy to cover for it.
- [[Why a big chi-square means a poor fit]]: A big χ² means a poor fit because χ² is built by adding up the (squared, scaled) gaps between observed and expected; big gaps make a big total.
- [[Why a bigger sample gives a clearer answer]]: A bigger sample gives a clearer answer because chance wobble shrinks relative to the total, so real differences stand out and fake ones fade.
- [[Why a dihybrid cross gives 9 3 3 1]]: A dihybrid cross gives 9:3:3:1 because each gene separately gives 3:1, and when the genes sort independently, you multiply the two 3:1 splits.
- [[Why a gamete carries only one allele of each gene]]: A gamete carries one allele of each gene because meiosis separates the two homologous chromosomes into different cells, and each gamete gets just one of the pair.
- [[Why a test cross uses a homozygous recessive partner]]: A test cross uses an aa partner because aa can only give a, so it adds nothing that hides the mystery parent's alleles; every offspring's phenotype directly shows which allele came from the mystery parent.
- [[Why about 1 in 20 honest tables will still reject]]: About 1 in 20 honest tables will still reject because the 0.05 level means: if H₀ is true, chance alone pushes χ² past the critical value 5% of the time.
- [[Why an albino seedling means both parents carry g]]: An albino seedling (gg) means both parents carry g because a seedling gets exactly one allele from each parent; to have two g's, each parent must have given one.
- [[Why chi-square needs counts not percentages]]: Chi-square needs raw counts because the size of χ² depends on how many things you counted; percentages throw away the sample size, so the test can't judge how much wobble to expect.
- [[Why codominance and incomplete dominance are different]]: In incomplete dominance the heterozygote is in between (pink: a halfway amount of one product).
- [[Why degrees of freedom are categories minus one]]: Degrees of freedom are categories minus one because once the total is fixed, the last category is forced: only the others are free to vary by chance.
- [[Why dominant does not mean common]]: Dominant describes how an allele behaves inside one person (does one copy show?), not how many people carry it.
- [[Why each birth is an independent event]]: Each birth is independent because every child comes from a fresh egg and a fresh sperm, made by meiosis that has no memory of earlier children.
- [[Why ear 5 uses a different critical value]]: Ear 5 uses a higher critical value (7.815) because it has four phenotype categories, so df = 3, and more categories give chance more room to raise χ².
- [[Why every box in a Punnett square is equally likely]]: Every box in a Punnett square is equally likely because each parent makes each kind of gamete equally often, and which egg meets which sperm is random.
- [[Why incomplete dominance is not blending]]: Incomplete dominance looks like blending (red × white → pink), but it isn't, because the original alleles come back unchanged in the next generation.
- [[Why mitochondria come only from the mother]]: Mitochondria come only from the mother because the egg supplies all the cytoplasm (and its mitochondria) to the zygote, while the sperm contributes almost nothing but its nucleus, and any sperm mitochondria that do get i
- [[Why one table can fit two hypotheses at once]]: One table can fit two hypotheses because with small samples, the wobble from chance is wide enough to cover more than one ratio.
- [[Why real counts never match the prediction exactly]]: Real counts almost never match the prediction exactly because each offspring is a separate chance event, and chance wobbles.
- [[Why the expected ratio must be chosen before counting]]: The expected ratio must be chosen before counting because if you pick it after seeing the data, you can always find some ratio that fits, and the test becomes meaningless.
- [[Why the null hypothesis says there is no difference]]: The null hypothesis says "no difference" because that's the one claim that makes an exact, checkable prediction: if it's true, you know precisely what counts to expect.
- [[Why the recessive trait comes back in the F2]]: The recessive trait vanishes in the F1 because every F1 has one dominant allele hiding the recessive one.
- [[Why the sickle cell allele survives]]: The sickle cell allele survives in malaria regions because carriers (HbᴬHbˢ) resist malaria better than people with two normal alleles, so carriers leave more children, keeping the allele common even though two copies ca
- [[Why the working allele is usually dominant]]: The working allele is usually dominant because one working copy usually makes enough of its protein to do the job, so the broken copy's absence goes unnoticed.
- [[Why two heterozygotes give a 3 to 1 ratio]]: Two heterozygotes give 3:1 because their four equally likely offspring boxes are GG, Gg, gG and gg, and three of the four contain at least one dominant allele.
- [[Why we compare chi-square with the critical value not with 0.05]]: We compare χ² with the critical value, not with 0.05, because they're different kinds of numbers: χ² is a mismatch score, 0.05 is a probability.
- [[Why we count five rows instead of the whole ear]]: We count five rows because that's a big enough sample (about 100-250 kernels) for a reliable chi-square, while leaving class time for the math.
- [[Why we divide by the expected count]]: We divide by E because a gap's importance depends on its size relative to what you expected: being off by 5 is huge if you expected 10, and trivial if you expected 1,000.
- [[Why we need a statistical test at all]]: We need a statistical test because our eyes can't judge whether a gap is chance or real; a test gives a fair, agreed rule so everyone reaches the same verdict from the same data.
- [[Why we never accept the null hypothesis]]: We never "accept" H₀ because a small χ² only shows the data is consistent with it, not that it's true; other hypotheses might fit just as well, and more data might reveal a difference.
- [[Why we square the gaps in chi-square]]: We square the gaps because the raw gaps (O − E) always add up to zero, so you have to remove the signs to measure the total mismatch, and squaring also makes big gaps count more than small ones.
- [[Why wrinkled kernels are sweet]]: Wrinkled kernels are sweet because hh kernels can't turn sugar into starch, so the sugar stays as sugar, which also pulls in water that later dries out, wrinkling the kernel.
- [[Why you have two copies of every gene]]: You have two copies of every gene (on autosomes) because you were built from two cells: one egg from your mother and one sperm from your father, each carrying one full set of chromosomes.

## Atomic notes · Basics from Zero (11)

- [[Decimals and Percentages]]: A decimal is a fraction with the division actually done (¾ = 0.75), and a percent is that decimal counted out of 100 (0.75 = 75%).
- [[Fractions and Ratios]]: A fraction is a part of a whole written as top over bottom (¾ means 3 parts out of 4), and a ratio compares groups to each other (3 : 1 means 3 of one kind for every 1 of the other).
- [[Greek Letters and Symbols]]: Three Greek letters appear in Lab 7: χ (chi, said "ky"), α (alpha) and Σ (capital sigma).
- [[Inequality Signs]]: The signs < and  say which of two numbers is bigger: 0.130 < 3.841 reads "0.130 is less than 3.841", and 7.348  3.841 reads "7.348 is greater than 3.841".
- [[Multiplying Fractions]]: To multiply two fractions, multiply the tops together and the bottoms together: ¾ × ¾ = 9/16.
- [[Order of Operations]]: When a formula has several steps, do brackets first, then powers (squares), then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.
- [[Reading a Data Table]]: In a data table, each row is one category (or one thing) and each column is one kind of information about it.
- [[Rounding and Decimal Places]]: Rounding means shortening a number to fewer decimal places, and the rule for this lab is: keep plenty of decimals while you work, and round only the final χ².
- [[Squares and Square Roots]]: Squaring a number means multiplying it by itself (4² = 4 × 4 = 16), and a square root undoes that (√16 = 4).
- [[Subscripts and Superscripts]]: Small letters or numbers written below a symbol (subscripts, like H₀ or F₁) or above it (superscripts, like Xᴮ or Iᴬ) are labels that tell you which one of something you mean.
- [[Summation Notation]]: The symbol Σ (the Greek capital letter sigma) means "add up all of these".

## Atomic notes · Building Blocks (26)

- [[Allele]]: An allele is one version of a gene; different alleles of the same gene differ slightly in their DNA and can give different traits.
- [[Amino Acid]]: Amino acids are the 20 small building blocks that cells string together, in an order set by a gene, to make proteins.
- [[Autosome]]: An autosome is any chromosome that isn't a sex chromosome; humans have 22 pairs of them.
- [[Chlorophyll]]: Chlorophyll is the green pigment that lets plants capture light energy for photosynthesis.
- [[Chromosome]]: A chromosome is one very long DNA molecule, packed tightly with proteins, carrying hundreds to thousands of genes in a fixed order.
- [[Cytoplasm]]: The cytoplasm is everything inside a cell outside the nucleus: the jelly-like fluid plus the organelles floating in it, including mitochondria.
- [[DNA]]: DNA is the molecule that stores an organism's instructions, written as a long sequence of four chemical "letters": A, T, C and G.
- [[Diploid]]: A diploid cell has two complete sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent, so it carries two copies of every gene.
- [[Enzyme]]: An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one particular chemical reaction, such as one step in making a pigment.
- [[Gene]]: A gene is a stretch of DNA at a fixed place on a chromosome that holds the instructions for one product, usually a protein, which then produces a trait.
- [[Gene Expression]]: Gene expression is the two-step process by which a cell reads a gene and builds its protein: DNA is copied into RNA (transcription), and RNA is read to string amino acids together (translation).
- [[Genotype]]: A genotype is the pair of alleles an individual carries for a gene, written as two letters such as RR, Rr or rr.
- [[Haploid]]: A haploid cell has just one set of chromosomes, so it carries one copy of each gene; eggs and sperm are haploid.
- [[Heredity]]: Heredity is the passing of traits from parents to offspring, and genetics is the science of how it works.
- [[Homologous Chromosomes]]: Homologous chromosomes are a matching pair, one from each parent, carrying the same genes in the same order, though possibly different alleles.
- [[Locus]]: A locus (plural loci, said LOH-kus and LOH-sigh) is the fixed address on a chromosome where a particular gene sits.
- [[Mutation]]: A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence; mutations are the original source of every new allele.
- [[Nucleus]]: The nucleus is the membrane-wrapped compartment of a plant or animal cell that holds its chromosomes.
- [[Organelle]]: An organelle is a specialized structure inside a cell that does a particular job, like an organ inside a body.
- [[Phenotype]]: A phenotype is what an individual actually looks like (or how it measures) for a trait, produced by its genotype together with its environment.
- [[Photosynthesis]]: Photosynthesis is how plants use light energy, carbon dioxide and water to make sugar, their food.
- [[Pigment]]: A pigment is a colored molecule: it absorbs some colors of light and reflects the rest, and the reflected light is the color you see.
- [[Protein]]: A protein is a chain of amino acids, built from a gene's instructions, that folds into a shape that lets it do one job.
- [[Sex Chromosomes]]: Sex chromosomes are the pair involved in determining biological sex: most human females are XX and most males are XY.
- [[Trait]]: A trait is a feature you can observe or measure, like kernel color, plant height or blood type.
- [[Variation]]: Variation means individuals of the same kind differ from one another, and genetic variation is the part of those differences caused by different alleles.

## Atomic notes · Meiosis and Gametes (13)

- [[Crossing Over]]: Crossing over is when two partner chromosomes swap matching segments during meiosis I, creating chromosomes with new combinations of alleles.
- [[Egg]]: The egg (ovum) is the female gamete: a large haploid cell carrying one set of chromosomes plus nearly all the cytoplasm, nutrients and mitochondria for the new embryo.
- [[Fertilization]]: Fertilization is the fusion of an egg and a sperm, which restores two copies of every chromosome and creates a zygote.
- [[Gamete]]: A gamete is a haploid reproductive cell, an egg or a sperm, carrying exactly one allele of each gene.
- [[Gene Linkage]]: Gene linkage is the tendency of genes that sit close together on the same chromosome to be inherited together, which breaks the independent-assortment rule.
- [[Genetic Recombination]]: Genetic recombination is the shuffling of alleles into new combinations in each generation, through crossing over, independent assortment and random fertilization.
- [[Law of Independent Assortment]]: The law of independent assortment says that alleles of genes on different chromosomes go into gametes independently of each other, so every combination is equally likely.
- [[Law of Segregation]]: The law of segregation says the two alleles of a gene separate when gametes form, so each gamete gets just one, chosen at random.
- [[Meiosis]]: Meiosis is the cell division that makes eggs and sperm: it separates each chromosome pair so that every gamete gets one chromosome of each pair, and so one allele of each gene.
- [[Oogenesis]]: Oogenesis is the making of eggs by meiosis, which puts almost all the cytoplasm into one big egg.
- [[Sperm]]: A sperm is the male gamete: a small, mobile haploid cell that delivers one set of chromosomes to the egg and almost nothing else.
- [[Spermatogenesis]]: Spermatogenesis is the making of sperm by meiosis: four equal, small, streamlined cells from each starting cell.
- [[Zygote]]: A zygote is the single cell formed when egg and sperm fuse: the first cell of a new individual, with one allele of each gene from each parent.

## Atomic notes · Alleles and Genotypes (14)

- [[Allele Notation]]: Allele notation writes each gene as one letter: capital for the dominant allele, the same letter in lowercase for the recessive one.
- [[Carrier]]: A carrier is a heterozygous individual who has one copy of a recessive allele (often a disease allele) but doesn't show the trait.
- [[Dominant Allele]]: A dominant allele is one whose trait shows even when there's only one copy of it: in a heterozygote, it hides the other allele.
- [[Gregor Mendel]]: Gregor Mendel was a monk in the 1860s who discovered the basic rules of inheritance by crossing pea plants and counting their offspring.
- [[Heterozygous]]: Heterozygous means carrying two different alleles of a gene, like Rr.
- [[Homozygous]]: Homozygous means carrying two identical alleles of a gene, like RR or rr.
- [[Homozygous Dominant]]: Homozygous dominant means carrying two copies of the dominant allele (like RR), so the individual shows the dominant phenotype and can only pass on the dominant allele.
- [[Homozygous Recessive]]: Homozygous recessive means carrying two copies of the recessive allele (like rr); it's the only genotype that shows the recessive phenotype.
- [[Hybrid]]: In genetics, a hybrid is the offspring of two different purebred parents, so it is heterozygous for the genes where they differ.
- [[Monogenic Trait]]: A monogenic ("monogenetic", single-gene) trait is controlled by one gene, so its phenotypes fall into a few clear categories.
- [[Pea Plant]]: The garden pea is the plant Mendel used, and the manual's practice problems use pea flower color (D/d) and height (H/h) in his honor.
- [[Polygenic Trait]]: A polygenic trait is shaped by many genes at once (often plus the environment), so it varies smoothly instead of falling into a few categories.
- [[Purebred]]: A purebred (true-breeding) plant or animal is homozygous for a trait, so when bred with its own kind it always produces offspring with that same trait.
- [[Recessive Allele]]: A recessive allele is one whose trait shows only when an individual has two copies of it, because any dominant allele present hides it.

## Atomic notes · Patterns of Inheritance (10)

- [[ABO Blood Type]]: ABO blood type is controlled by one gene with three alleles: Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant with each other, and both are dominant over i.
- [[Codominance]]: Codominance is the pattern where both alleles show fully and separately in the heterozygote, so both traits appear at once.
- [[Complete Dominance]]: Complete dominance is the pattern where one allele fully hides the other, so the heterozygote looks exactly like the dominant homozygote.
- [[Heterozygote Advantage]]: Heterozygote advantage is when heterozygotes survive and reproduce better than either homozygote, which keeps both alleles common in a population.
- [[Incomplete Dominance]]: Incomplete dominance is the pattern where neither allele fully wins, so the heterozygote is in between the two homozygotes.
- [[Multiple Alleles]]: Multiple alleles means a gene has more than two versions in the population, even though each individual still carries only two.
- [[Pattern of Inheritance]]: A pattern of inheritance is the rule for how two alleles combine into one phenotype, and how a trait passes from parents to children.
- [[Sex-Linked Inheritance]]: Sex-linked inheritance is the pattern of genes on a sex chromosome (usually the X), which are inherited differently by males and females.
- [[X-Linked Trait]]: An X-linked trait is controlled by a gene on the X chromosome; X-linked recessive traits show far more often in males because males have only one X.
- [[Y-Linked Trait]]: A Y-linked trait is controlled by a gene on the Y chromosome, so it occurs only in males and passes from father to every son.

## Atomic notes · Crosses and Predictions (21)

- [[1-1 Ratio]]: The 1 : 1 ratio is expected when a heterozygote is crossed with a homozygous recessive (Rr × rr): half dominant phenotype, half recessive.
- [[1-1-1-1 Ratio]]: The 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio is expected in a dihybrid test cross (RrHh × rrhh): all four phenotype combinations equally often.
- [[1-2-1 Ratio]]: The 1 : 2 : 1 ratio is the genotype ratio of two heterozygotes (1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr), and also the phenotype ratio under incomplete dominance.
- [[3-1 Ratio]]: The 3 : 1 ratio is the phenotype ratio when two heterozygotes are crossed under complete dominance: ¾ dominant, ¼ recessive.
- [[9-3-3-1 Ratio]]: The 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio is the phenotype ratio from crossing two double heterozygotes (RrHh × RrHh) when the genes assort independently.
- [[Controlled Cross]]: A controlled cross is a mating where the breeder decides exactly which two parents mate, so the offspring's parents are known.
- [[Cross]]: A cross is a planned mating between two individuals; "Gg × gg" is read "Gg crossed with gg".
- [[Dihybrid Cross]]: A dihybrid cross follows two genes at once, classically two double heterozygotes (RrHh × RrHh), and gives a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotype ratio.
- [[F1 Generation]]: The F₁ (first filial) generation is the offspring of the P generation; from two different purebreds, every F₁ is heterozygous and they all look alike.
- [[F2 Generation]]: The F₂ (second filial) generation is the offspring of two F₁ individuals crossed together; the hidden recessive trait reappears in about a quarter of them.
- [[FOIL Method]]: FOIL (First, Outer, Inner, Last) is a shortcut for listing the four gametes of a double heterozygote like RrHh: RH, Rh, rH, rh.
- [[Genotype Frequency]]: Genotype frequency is the fraction (or percent) of offspring expected to have a given genotype: its number of Punnett boxes divided by the total.
- [[Genotypic Ratio]]: A genotypic ratio compares how many offspring of each genotype a cross is expected to give, like 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr.
- [[Monohybrid Cross]]: A monohybrid cross follows one gene, classically two heterozygotes (Rr × Rr).
- [[P Generation]]: The P (parental) generation is the pair of parents that starts a breeding experiment, classically two purebreds like RR × rr.
- [[Pedigree]]: A pedigree is a family-tree diagram that follows a trait through generations: squares are males, circles are females, and filled symbols are affected.
- [[Phenotype Frequency]]: Phenotype frequency is the fraction (or percent) of offspring expected to show a given phenotype, found by grouping Punnett boxes that look alike.
- [[Phenotypic Ratio]]: A phenotypic ratio compares how many offspring of each phenotype a cross is expected to give, like 3 purple : 1 yellow.
- [[Punnett Square]]: A Punnett square is a grid with one parent's possible gametes across the top and the other's down the side, so each box is one equally likely offspring.
- [[Self-Pollination]]: Self-pollination is fertilizing a plant with its own pollen, which crosses a plant with itself.
- [[Test Cross]]: A test cross mates an individual that shows the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive, to reveal whether it's homozygous or heterozygous.

## Atomic notes · Human Genetic Disorders (19)

- [[Achondroplasia]]: Achondroplasia is an autosomal dominant disorder of bone growth and the most common form of dwarfism.
- [[Anemia]]: Anemia is a shortage of healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin, so tissues get too little oxygen.
- [[Autosomal Dominant Disorder]]: An autosomal dominant disorder is caused by an allele on an autosome that causes disease even in one copy (Aa or AA).
- [[Autosomal Recessive Disorder]]: An autosomal recessive disorder is caused by an allele on an autosome that has to be present in two copies (aa) for someone to be affected.
- [[Color Blindness]]: Red-green color blindness is an X-linked recessive trait in which one type of color sensor (cone) in the eye doesn't work normally.
- [[Cystic Fibrosis]]: Cystic fibrosis is an autosomal recessive disorder in which a faulty salt channel makes mucus thick and sticky, clogging the lungs and gut.
- [[Evolution]]: Evolution is the change in how common alleles are in a population over generations.
- [[Genetic Disorder]]: A genetic disorder is a disease caused by an allele that doesn't work properly; someone who has it is "affected", someone who doesn't is "unaffected" or "normal".
- [[Hemoglobin]]: Hemoglobin is the protein that packs red blood cells and carries oxygen around the body.
- [[Hemophilia]]: Hemophilia is an X-linked recessive disorder in which a blood-clotting factor is missing, so bleeding is hard to stop.
- [[Huntington Disease]]: Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant disorder in which brain cells slowly die, usually starting in mid-life.
- [[Malaria]]: Malaria is a disease caused by a parasite (Plasmodium), spread by mosquitoes, that lives inside red blood cells.
- [[Marfan Syndrome]]: Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder of connective tissue, causing tall stature, long limbs and heart and blood-vessel problems.
- [[Natural Selection]]: Natural selection is the process where individuals whose alleles help them survive and reproduce in their environment leave more offspring, so those alleles become more common.
- [[Neurofibromatosis]]: Neurofibromatosis is an autosomal dominant disorder in which benign tumors grow on nerves.
- [[Phenylketonuria]]: Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an autosomal recessive disorder in which the body can't break down phenylalanine, an amino acid in food; a special diet from birth prevents the damage.
- [[Sickle Cell Anemia]]: Sickle cell anemia is an autosomal recessive disorder (Hbˢ Hbˢ) in which abnormal hemoglobin bends red cells into stiff crescents that block small blood vessels and break down early.
- [[Sickle Cell Trait]]: Sickle cell trait is the carrier state, Hbᴬ Hbˢ: the person makes both normal and sickle hemoglobin, is usually healthy, and resists malaria.
- [[Tay-Sachs Disease]]: Tay-Sachs disease is an autosomal recessive disorder in which a missing enzyme lets a fatty substance pile up in nerve cells and destroy them.

## Atomic notes · Mitochondrial DNA (14)

- [[ATP]]: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the small molecule cells use as ready energy, like cash in a wallet.
- [[Aerobic Respiration]]: Aerobic respiration is the oxygen-using breakdown of food in mitochondria, which releases energy and stores it as ATP.
- [[Endosymbiotic Theory]]: Endosymbiotic theory says mitochondria are descended from oxygen-using bacteria that were swallowed by an ancient cell and kept alive inside it.
- [[Eukaryote]]: A eukaryote is an organism whose cells keep their DNA in a nucleus and contain organelles like mitochondria; plants, animals and fungi are eukaryotes.
- [[Forensic Identification]]: Forensic identification uses DNA to identify people; mtDNA helps because it can be matched to any maternal relative and survives in damaged samples.
- [[Genealogy]]: Genealogy is tracing family history, and mtDNA lets it follow the direct female line far back in time.
- [[Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy]]: Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON, said LAY-ber) is an mtDNA disease, inherited from the mother, in which optic-nerve cells die and central vision is suddenly lost, usually in young adults.
- [[Maternal Inheritance]]: Maternal inheritance means something is passed only from mother to offspring, as mitochondria and their DNA are.
- [[Mitochondrial DNA]]: Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is the small circular DNA inside mitochondria; in humans it holds 37 genes and comes only from the mother.
- [[Mitochondrion]]: A mitochondrion (plural mitochondria) is the organelle that burns food with oxygen to make ATP, the cell's energy currency.
- [[Nonsyndromic Deafness]]: Nonsyndromic deafness is hearing loss with no other symptoms; some forms come from mtDNA mutations passed down by the mother.
- [[Prokaryote]]: A prokaryote is a single-celled organism without a nucleus, such as a bacterium; its DNA is usually one circular loop.
- [[rRNA]]: rRNA (ribosomal RNA) forms the core of the ribosome, the machine that builds proteins.
- [[tRNA]]: tRNA (transfer RNA) carries amino acids to the ribosome and matches each one to the right spot while a protein is built.

## Atomic notes · Probability and Chance (12)

- [[Frequency]]: Frequency in this lab means how often something shows up, as a fraction of the total: the same idea as a proportion.
- [[Independent Event]]: Two events are independent when the result of one doesn't change the odds of the other.
- [[Law of Large Numbers]]: The law of large numbers says that as you repeat a chance event more times, the observed share gets closer to the true probability.
- [[Percent]]: A percent is a proportion written out of 100: multiply by 100 and add %.
- [[Population]]: A population is the whole group you want to know about.
- [[Probability]]: Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is: 0 means never, 1 means always, ½ means half the time.
- [[Product Rule]]: The product rule says: the chance of two independent things both happening is their chances multiplied.
- [[Proportion]]: A proportion is one part divided by the whole, a fraction between 0 and 1.
- [[Random Chance]]: Random chance is the unpredictable part of an outcome: you know the odds, but not which way any single event will go.
- [[Ratio]]: A ratio compares the sizes of groups to each other, written with colons, like 3:1.
- [[Sample]]: A sample is the part of the population you actually count.
- [[Sample Size]]: Sample size is how many things you counted, written n.

## Atomic notes · Statistics and Chi-Square (27)

- [[A Priori Hypothesis]]: An a priori hypothesis is one you fix before you see the data.
- [[Alternative Hypothesis]]: The alternative hypothesis (H₁) is the opposite of the null: the gap between observed and expected is too large to be chance, so something other than the predicted ratio is going on.
- [[Assumptions of Chi-Square]]: The chi-square test only works properly when its assumptions hold:
- [[Categories]]: Categories are the separate bins you sort your counts into: one row of the chi-square grid each.
- [[Chi-Square Distribution]]: The chi-square distribution is the shape you'd get if you repeated an experiment many times with H₀ true and plotted all the χ² values.
- [[Chi-Square Statistic]]: The chi-square statistic is the single number the test produces: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E.
- [[Chi-Square Test]]: The chi-square (χ², said "kai-square") test checks whether observed counts are close enough to expected counts that the difference could be chance.
- [[Critical Value]]: The critical value is the cut-off χ² for your df at the 0.05 level: if your χ² is bigger, reject H₀; if smaller, keep it.
- [[Critical Value Table]]: The critical value table lists cut-off χ² values by degrees of freedom (rows) and significance level (columns).
- [[Degrees of Freedom]]: Degrees of freedom (df) is how many categories are free to vary once you know the total: categories − 1.
- [[Expected Value]]: An expected value (E) is the count you'd get if H₀ were exactly true: expected fraction × total counted.
- [[Fail to Reject the Null Hypothesis]]: You fail to reject the null hypothesis when χ² is smaller than the critical value: the gap is small enough to be chance, so the data is consistent with the predicted ratio.
- [[Goodness of Fit]]: Goodness of fit is how well observed counts match the counts a hypothesis predicts.
- [[Hypothesis]]: A hypothesis is a clear, testable guess about how something works, made before you look at the results.
- [[Level of Significance]]: The level of significance (α) is how much risk of a false alarm you're willing to take; biology usually uses 0.05, which is 5%.
- [[Null Hypothesis]]: The null hypothesis (H₀) is the "nothing special is going on" claim: the observed counts differ from the expected ones only by chance.
- [[Observed Value]]: An observed value (O) is the count you actually got: the number of seedlings or kernels you saw in one category.
- [[P-Value]]: The p-value is the chance of getting a gap at least as big as yours if H₀ were true.
- [[Reject the Null Hypothesis]]: You reject the null hypothesis when χ² is larger than the critical value: the gap is too big to blame on chance, so the predicted ratio is probably wrong.
- [[Results Statement]]: A results statement is the one or two sentences that report a chi-square result: the numbers, the decision, and what it means biologically.
- [[Retained Null Hypothesis]]: A retained null hypothesis is one you kept because χ² fell below the critical value; it's another way to say "failed to reject".
- [[Statistical Analysis]]: Statistical analysis is using numbers to decide whether a pattern in data is real or could just be chance.
- [[Statistically Significant]]: Statistically significant means the gap between observed and expected is too large to be explained by chance alone (χ²  critical value).
- [[Summation]]: Summation means adding up a list of numbers; the symbol Σ (capital Greek sigma) means "add up all of these".
- [[Test Statistic]]: A test statistic is one number calculated from your data that sums up how far it is from what H₀ predicts.
- [[Type I Error]]: A Type I error is rejecting H₀ when it was actually true: a false alarm.
- [[Type II Error]]: A Type II error is failing to reject H₀ when it was actually false: missing a real difference.

## Atomic notes · The Corn Lab (19)

- [[Albino Seedling]]: An albino seedling is gg: it has no working copy of the chlorophyll gene, so it's white and can't photosynthesize.
- [[Anthocyanin]]: Anthocyanin is the purple-red pigment that colors purple corn kernels (and blueberries, red cabbage and grapes).
- [[Corn]]: Corn (maize, Zea mays) is the plant Lab 7 uses because each ear holds hundreds of offspring from a single cross, all visible at once.
- [[Corn Ear]]: A corn ear is the female flower cluster of the corn plant; after pollination each ovule on it becomes a kernel, so a mature ear is a whole family of offspring from one cross.
- [[Counting Five Rows]]: In Lab 7 each student counts the kernels in five rows of one ear (not the whole cob), giving a sample of about 100-250 kernels.
- [[Green Seedling]]: A green seedling has at least one working G allele (GG or Gg), so it makes chlorophyll.
- [[Kernel]]: A kernel is one corn seed: a single offspring, made when one pollen grain fertilized one ovule.
- [[Lab 7 Letter Key]]: The Lab 7 letter key: G/g = green/albino seedlings; R/r = purple/yellow kernels; H/h = smooth (starchy)/wrinkled (sugary) kernels.
- [[Ovule]]: An ovule is the structure on the ear that holds the egg; once fertilized, it grows into a kernel.
- [[Pollen]]: Pollen is the dust-like powder from the tassel; each grain carries the plant's male gametes (sperm).
- [[Purple Kernel]]: A purple kernel has at least one R allele (RR or Rr), so it makes the purple pigment anthocyanin.
- [[Seedling Tray]]: The seedling tray is two six-pack pots per table, about 24 corn seedlings in total, all from the same parental cross.
- [[Silk]]: A silk is one of the long threads coming out of the top of the ear; each one is a tube leading to a single ovule.
- [[Smooth Kernel]]: A smooth kernel has at least one H allele (HH or Hh), so it turns sugar into starch and stays plump when it dries.
- [[Starch]]: Starch is a long chain of sugar molecules joined together; plants use it to store energy compactly.
- [[Sugar]]: Sugar is a small, sweet molecule plants make by photosynthesis; it's the building block of starch.
- [[Tassel]]: The tassel is the male flower cluster at the top of the corn plant; it makes pollen.
- [[Wrinkled Kernel]]: A wrinkled kernel is hh: it can't turn sugar into starch, so it holds sugar and water, then shrivels as it dries.
- [[Yellow Kernel]]: A yellow kernel is rr: no purple pigment, so the yellow underneath shows through.
