**The big question:** How do the two alleles a parent has get passed on, and why does each child get exactly one from each parent?

## The story in plain words

You have two copies of every chromosome. To make a baby, you can't hand over both copies, or every generation would double. So your body makes special half-cells, [[Gamete|gametes]], by [[Meiosis|meiosis]], which deals out **one chromosome of each pair** into each gamete, like dealing one card of each matching pair.

If you're **Gg**, half your gametes get the G card and half get the g card ([[Law of Segregation]]). If you track two genes on different chromosome pairs, the dealing of one pair doesn't affect the other, so all four combinations are equally likely ([[Law of Independent Assortment]]).

Then [[Fertilization|fertilization]] is a random meeting: any egg can meet any sperm. The [[Zygote|zygote]] gets one card from each parent and is [[Diploid|diploid]] again.

## Build it in order

1. [[Diploid]] → [[Haploid]] → [[Homologous Chromosomes]]
2. [[Meiosis]] → [[Gamete]] → [[Egg]] → [[Sperm]]
3. [[Law of Segregation]] → [[Law of Independent Assortment]]
4. [[Fertilization]] → [[Zygote]]
5. Extras: [[Crossing Over]], [[Genetic Recombination]], [[Oogenesis]], [[Spermatogenesis]]

## Bridge to the Punnett square

A [[Punnett Square|Punnett square]] is just this story written as a table: gametes on the outside (one allele each), zygotes inside (two alleles each). The TA tip: put Mom's eggs across the top and Dad's sperm down the side.

Try it yourself. Why can a gamete never be 'Gg'? Meiosis separates the homologous chromosomes, so each gamete gets only one of the pair, hence one allele.
