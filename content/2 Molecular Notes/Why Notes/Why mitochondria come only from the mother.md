Mitochondria come only from the mother because the egg supplies all the cytoplasm (and its mitochondria) to the zygote, while the sperm contributes almost nothing but its nucleus, and any sperm mitochondria that do get in are destroyed.

Size tells most of it: a human egg is one of the largest cells in the body, full of hundreds of thousands of mitochondria. A sperm is a tiny swimmer: a nucleus, a tail, and a few mitochondria in the neck to power the swim. At fertilization the egg tags the sperm's mitochondria and breaks them down (manual p. 123).

![[Mitochondrial DNA follows the mother.png|700]]

*mtDNA passes from a mother to all her children, but only daughters pass it on.*

Result: your mitochondria, and their DNA, are copies of your mother's, her mother's, and so on back. That's what makes mtDNA useful for [[Genealogy]] and [[Forensic Identification]].

Try it yourself. A man has an mtDNA disease. Can his children inherit it from him? No.
