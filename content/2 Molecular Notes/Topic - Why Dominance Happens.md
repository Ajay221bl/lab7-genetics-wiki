> [!abstract] The big question
> Why does one allele 'hide' another? Is the dominant allele stronger somehow?

## The story in plain words

Feynman liked to say that a name isn't understanding. 'Dominant' is a name. Here's the mechanism.

Most genes are recipes for an [[Enzyme|enzyme]]: a machine that does a job. Most 'recessive' alleles are **broken** recipes: their machine doesn't work.

- **RR**: two working recipes → lots of machines → purple.
- **Rr**: one working recipe → still enough machines → **just as purple**. The working allele looks 'dominant' simply because it's the one doing anything. → [[Complete Dominance]]
- **rr**: no working recipe → no paint → yellow. The broken allele only shows when there's no working copy. → [[Recessive Allele]]

Now tweak the story:
- If **one** working copy makes only half the paint, and half isn't enough for full color, the heterozygote is in between: pink, gray, wavy. → [[Incomplete Dominance]]
- If **both** alleles work but make different products (one adds an A sugar tag, the other a B tag), both products are present side by side. → [[Codominance]]

So dominance isn't about strength; it's about what the proteins do and how much is enough.

## Build it in order

1. [[Enzyme]] → [[Allele]] → [[Mutation]]
2. [[Dominant Allele]] → [[Recessive Allele]] → [[Allele Notation]]
3. [[Complete Dominance]] → [[Incomplete Dominance]] → [[Codominance]]

## A myth to drop

'Dominant means more common.' No. [[Huntington Disease]] is caused by a dominant allele and is rare; yellow kernels' r is recessive yet common in many corn lines.

> [!question]- Check yourself: Predict the color of a kernel if one working R copy made only 30% of the pigment needed for full purple.
> **Answer:** Pale purple (intermediate): that's incomplete dominance.
